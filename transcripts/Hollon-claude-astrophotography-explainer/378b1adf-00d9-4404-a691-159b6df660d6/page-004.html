## P4 · Flats (page 1)

**Eyebrow:** Chapter 4 · Calibration

**Title:** Flats, or how to even out the field

**Lede:** Not every pixel on the sensor receives the same amount of light, even when the sky in front of the telescope is perfectly even. A flat measures that unevenness so it can be divided out of every light frame.

**Full-width illustration:** *How a flat corrects* — light frame ÷ master flat = corrected light (Illustrations page).

### The problem you can see

*(An ROI image sits beside this section: the dust mote in one raw frame, as the app will show it.)*

Point a telescope at a perfectly uniform patch of sky and the picture that comes out will not be uniform. The corners come out darker than the centre, because the optics deliver less light to the edge of the field than to the middle; this falling-off is called vignetting. And anything sitting on the glass in front of the sensor, most often a speck of dust, casts a soft, out-of-focus shadow that stays in the same place from one exposure to the next. Our sensor has one such dust mote on the left side, and once you know where to look you can find it as a faint dark ring in every raw frame.

Neither of these is noise. They are perfectly repeatable, and that is exactly what makes them correctable.

### A flat is a picture of the unevenness

A flat frame is an exposure of an evenly lit surface, taken through the same telescope, with the same camera, the same filter, the same focus and the same dust. At Stella Venator the even surface is a flat panel placed over the front of the telescope. Since the light going in was even, everything uneven in the picture that comes out must belong to the optics and the sensor: the vignetting, the dust shadow, and the small differences in how sensitive each pixel is.

That makes the flat a map of how much light each pixel actually receives. The correction that follows is a division, not a subtraction. Each pixel in the light frame is divided by the same pixel in the flat, after the flat has been scaled so that its typical value is one. A pixel the flat says is receiving 80 percent of the light is divided by 0.8 and comes back up to full strength; a pixel receiving all of it is divided by one and left alone.

> **Why?** — *Why can't the flat be drawn as another layer to peel away?* A flat scales each pixel rather than adding or removing a fixed amount, so how much it changes a pixel depends on how bright that pixel was to begin with. A dim corner is lifted a lot; a bright star in the same corner is lifted by the same factor, not the same amount.

### Why the flat needs its own dark frames

A flat is a photograph like any other. Besides the light from the panel, it records the small amount of signal the camera produces on its own during the exposure, which sits underneath everything as a constant offset. Divide by a flat that still carries that offset and the map is wrong in a specific way: the offset props up the dark corners of the flat more, in proportion, than it props up the bright centre, so the flat under-reports how uneven the field really is and the correction falls short. The dust shadow fades but does not quite go.

The fix is a set of dark flats: exposures of exactly the same length as the flats, with no light reaching the sensor, so they contain only what the camera adds by itself. Subtract each dark flat from its flat before the flats are combined into the master flat, and the map is honest again.

`[VERIFY: SPEC §7 P4 describes the uncalibrated flat as over-correcting toward the corners. With a dark-subtracted light and the flat's own offset left in, the expected effect is under-correction. Check the sign of the vignetting residual in flat_check.json before the lesson ships.]`

### Try it

## P5 · Flats, continued

**Eyebrow:** Chapter 4 · Calibration

**Title:** How bright should a flat be?

**Lede:** A flat has to be bright enough to measure the unevenness cleanly and dim enough that no pixel runs out of room. The usual advice is to aim for the middle of the camera's range, and this page shows why.

**Full-width illustration:** *Three flat exposures* — three histograms side by side: a hump near the left edge (10%), a hump in the middle (50%), a hump near the right edge (85%). (Illustrations page.)

### The histogram is the tool for this

A histogram counts how many pixels have each brightness. For a flat, which is a picture of an evenly lit panel, nearly all the pixels share similar values, so the histogram is a single hump. Where that hump sits tells you how bright the flat is.

### Too dim

If the hump sits near the left edge, the flat was a short exposure with little light in it. The unevenness is still there, but it is small in absolute terms and the random noise in each pixel is large by comparison. Dividing by a noisy map adds that noise to every light frame. The dust shadow is corrected, but roughly.

### Too bright

If the hump sits near the right edge, most pixels are close to the top of the camera's range. Nothing is clipped in the 85% flats used here, but the sensor's response is least predictable near the top, and the small dust shadow becomes a small difference between two large numbers. A flat at this level corrects the vignetting well and the mote less well. `[VERIFY against flat_check.json: AGENTS.md records that the 85% flat left the mote visible in a quick test. Do not say the 85% flats are saturated; they are not.]`

### In the middle

With the hump in the middle, every pixel has plenty of signal above the noise and plenty of room below the top. That is the flat we used on the previous page, and it is the one that removes the mote cleanly.

## P6 · Alignment

**Eyebrow:** Chapter 5 · Putting frames together

**Title:** Lining the frames up before averaging them

**Lede:** No two exposures land the stars on exactly the same pixels. Before frames can be averaged, each one has to be shifted, and if necessary rotated, so that the same star sits on the same pixel in every frame. That step is called registration, or alignment.

**Full-width illustration:** *Two frames, three stars* — the same star trio drawn in two frames offset by a few dozen pixels, with arrows showing the shift that brings them together. (Illustrations page; offset vectors from the Stage A star catalogues.)

### Why frames drift

Between exposures the mount is nudged on purpose by a few pixels so that hot pixels and dust do not pile up in the same place, a practice called dithering, and the mount also wanders a little on its own. Over a night the drift adds up. In this data set two frames from the same half of the night are about forty-seven pixels apart, and the frames from the second half of the night are rotated a full half-turn relative to the first, because the telescope flipped to the other side of its mount as the galaxy crossed the meridian.

### What alignment does

The software finds the same stars in every frame, works out the shift and rotation that map each frame onto one chosen reference frame, and resamples every frame onto the reference's pixel grid. After that, pixel 2,000 across and 1,500 down means the same patch of sky in every frame. The app uses the alignment PixInsight computed for these frames rather than computing its own.

Alignment moves whole frames. It does not sharpen anything: a star that trailed during an exposure is still trailed after alignment, only in the right place.

### Then the averaging can work

Average two aligned frames and the stars, which are in the same place in both, come out as they were. The random noise, which is different in each, partly cancels. The background gets smoother while the galaxy does not get fainter, and that is the whole trade. With two frames the noise should fall to about seven-tenths of a single frame's, which is one over the square root of two; the app measures it rather than assuming it.

## P7 · Algorithms

**Eyebrow:** Chapter 6 · Combining frames

**Title:** Five ways to combine a stack of pixels

**Lede:** Once the frames are aligned, every pixel position has a small stack of values, one from each frame. The combining method decides how those values become one number. The methods differ in what they do when one value is nothing like the others.

**Full-width illustration:** *One pixel's stack* — a column of five dots on a number line, four clustered and one far above; the five methods drawn as what they do to that column. (Illustrations page.)

### The problem in one pixel

Take a pixel where a satellite crossed in one frame out of five. Four frames say the sky is dim; one says it is bright. Whatever we do with that one value is what the finished image will show at that pixel.

### Average

Add the values and divide by how many there are. The average uses every frame, so it gives the smoothest background of any method, and it is also the most trusting: the one bright value pulls the result up, and the trail survives, fainter but visible. With four frames or fewer, the average is often still the best choice, because the other methods do not have enough values to judge which one is odd.

### Median

Sort the values and take the middle one. A single wild value cannot move the middle, so the trail simply disappears. The cost is noise: the median of five values wobbles more than their average, by roughly a quarter. For small stacks with an obvious outlier, that is often a trade worth making.

### Kappa-sigma clipping

Measure how spread out the values are, using the standard deviation, then throw away anything more than a chosen number of standard deviations from the middle, and average what is left. Repeat until nothing more is thrown away. The catch is that the outlier is included when the spread is measured, and one very bright value makes the spread large enough to hide inside. With five frames a single outlier can never be more than about two and a quarter standard deviations from the middle, so a limit of three never fires. This method needs roughly eight to fifteen frames to work as intended.

### Winsorized sigma clipping

The same idea, with one change in how the spread is measured. Before measuring, the most extreme values are pulled in to the edge of the normal range rather than left where they are, so an outlier cannot inflate its own alibi. Then it rejects and averages as above. It works from about eight frames and is excellent from fifteen up.

### Robust Chauvenet rejection

Instead of a fixed limit, ask a probability question: given this many frames, how likely is it that a value this far from the middle would appear by chance? If the answer is too unlikely, remove the single most improbable value and ask again. The questions get sharper over three rounds, first using robust estimates of the middle and the spread and finally the ordinary ones. It is the most careful of the five and wants fifteen frames or more. The version here follows PixInsight's, with the same default threshold. `[UNVERIFIED specifics stay out of the copy; see docs/knowledge/wbpp.md §4.6.]`

### Choosing

| Frames | Reasonable choices |
|---|---|
| 2–4 | Average, or median if one frame is clearly bad |
| 5–7 | Median, or winsorized clipping |
| 8–15 | Kappa-sigma or winsorized clipping |
| 15+ | Winsorized clipping or robust Chauvenet |

These are starting points, not rules. The workbench lets you try each one on the same frames.

## P8 · Light frames (page 1)

**Eyebrow:** Chapter 7 · Choosing frames

**Title:** Not every exposure deserves a place in the stack

**Lede:** A stack is only as good as the frames that go into it. Three measurements help you judge each one, and none of them is a substitute for looking.

### Three numbers per frame

**Star size.** The software fits a smooth profile to each star and reports its width at half its peak brightness, called the FWHM, in pixels. Smaller means sharper: steadier air, better focus, or both. Frames with much larger star sizes than the rest will blur the stack.

**Star count.** How many stars the software could detect and measure. Fewer stars than the other frames usually means something hid them: thin cloud, or stars smeared into trails that the detector no longer recognises as stars.

**Background histogram.** The shape of the pixel-brightness histogram. A frame with cloud in it has a lifted, wider background than its neighbours, even when the star count barely changes.
