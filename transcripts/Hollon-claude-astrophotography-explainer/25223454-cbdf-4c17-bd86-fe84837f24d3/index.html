That is off target. Evaluate the prompt on it's own merits

Review of the hackathon prompt

The vision is clear and the teaching approach is strong. The problem is that agents will follow it literally, and in several places it contradicts itself or leaves out decisions they would have to guess at. A few technical phrasings would also end up in the lesson text as inaccurate teaching. I've ordered the issues by how much damage they'd do.

1. Contradictions (these will produce wrong behavior)

  1. Caching. One line says "no authentication, caching, or other non-hackathon style features". A later line requires caching in localStorage that is "purged on page refresh". localStorage survives a refresh, and it tops out around 5–10 MB, so it can't hold images anyway. Suggested wording: "keep computed results in an in-memory cache keyed by the full settings; it's lost on reload."
  2. ROIs. "Select up to 4 ROI points" conflicts with "ROI positions are fixed and the user may not change them." Say "four fixed ROIs."
  3. The lesson list doesn't cover your second goal. Light selection and stacking are one of your two main goals. But the page list stops at Calibration, with no pages for exposure time vs. count, SNR, frame selection or rejection algorithms. Either the list is incomplete or the workbench challenges are meant to teach those topics. Say which.
  4. Which calibration frames exist. The data section lists flats at 10%, 50% and 85% with matching dark flats. The calibration section says "right now" there's only a 50% flat and that other exposure levels are for later. It also says "a dark flat" (singular). Meanwhile the "Flats – Continued" page needs the 10% and 85% sets. Decide whether those sets are in scope, and whether that's for the lesson only or the workbench too.
  5. Stretch rules. "Calibration images get their own individual stretch" conflicts with "Flats should not be stretched", because flats are calibration images. Also, a dust mote in an unstretched flat is only a few percent dip and nearly invisible. You probably mean "no AutoSTF; use a linear contrast stretch." Spell out what a flat should look like on screen.
  6. Frame identity. Several things are unclear here:
  7. "Frame 3" could mean file FRAME_0003 or the third frame.
  8. The synthetic frames are variants of frames 11 and 14–16. Does the user see 20 frames or 24? Can the clean and disaster versions of frame 11 both be selected? Stacking both counts the same noise twice.
  9. The Default scenario says it excludes frames with "synthetic" problems. The satellite trail is real, so is that frame in or out?
  10. Scope vs. requirements. The prompt says to cut anything not explicitly called for, then asks for 5 algorithms, 5 scenarios, 2 export formats, a set of precomputed flat masters, system-diagram SVGs and Docker. That isn't a contradiction, but it needs a priority order so agents know what to drop first.

2. Technical accuracy (you're judged on depth, and agents will copy this wording into the prose)

  1. Calibration removes fixed patterns, not noise. Several phrases treat these as the same thing: "what comprises the noise of a light image and how each calibration frame removes it", bias as "baseline noise", flats as "noise from the environment".
  2. Bias, dark signal, hot pixels, amp glow, vignetting and dust are repeatable patterns, and calibration subtracts or divides them out.
  3. Random noise (shot noise, read noise) can only be averaged down by stacking. Calibration actually adds a little noise, which is why masters are built from many frames.
  4. This distinction is the core idea of the lesson, and it fits your peeling-layers SVG: peel away the patterns and random noise is left, which leads straight into the stacking lesson.
  5. Also show that bias and darks are subtracted while flats divide.
  6. The calibration math isn't written down. "Applied in the correct order" isn't enough when every frame can be toggled independently. Write a formula table covering:
  7. whether the master dark includes bias;
  8. what happens if the user selects bias and dark (double subtraction?);
  9. flat minus dark flat vs. flat minus bias;
  10. how the master flat is normalized;
  11. flats without darks or bias. This causes the classic over-correction ring, which is a teaching moment worth naming.

"Each permutation of flats: with/without bias, dark or dark flats" is also ambiguous. A 300 s dark on a flat is wrong, and bias plus dark flat subtracts bias twice. List the permutations explicitly (flat level × {raw, −bias, −dark flat}). 3. Order of calibration and alignment. Calibration has to happen in sensor coordinates, before alignment, because dust and hot pixels stay fixed to the sensor while the stars move. The prompt offers light_registered as the stacking input but never says whether those files were calibrated first. (I checked: they were aligned from uncalibrated lights.) State the pipeline: raw → calibrate → apply stored alignment → normalize → reject → combine. 4. Normalization is missing. Sky brightness changes over the night, and clouds change it further. Without normalization before rejection, sigma clipping throws out large amounts of good data. Add it, and state that frame weighting is out of scope. 5. The challenges may not be solvable as described. A single outlier among n frames can be at most about √n standard deviations from the median, however bright it is. So κ=3 sigma clipping can't reject even a blazing satellite trail with fewer than about 10 frames. - Tracking challenge (3 frames): the bad frame reaches at most 1.7σ, so nothing gets rejected. - Cloud challenge (3 cloudy + 2 clear): the clouds are the majority, so median picks cloudy values. No per-pixel rejection can fix that, and only deselecting frames works.

That may be exactly the lesson you want, but write down each challenge's intended fix (its win condition) and check it offline before building the UI. 6. Median as the default. For clean Gaussian noise, the median is about 25% noisier than the mean. A 16-frame median is worth roughly 10 averaged frames. Presenting that as the default quietly teaches the wrong best practice. Winsorized sigma clipping would be the textbook default here. 7. Robust Chauvenet rejection is the hardest of the five algorithms to get right in a day. Cite the source (Maples et al. 2018) or accept a simplified version. Also say whether users can tune κ and the iteration count, because the "tight feedback loop" goal suggests they should. 8. "Mean well depth" should be "full well capacity."

3. Gaps that force agents to guess

  • Stretch baseline: is it computed from the raw reference frame or from the reference with the current calibration applied? If it's computed once and the user then removes bias, the image washes out. That's either a bug or a lesson; decide which.
  • Changing the reference frame: does it only change the display and stretch, or does it re-align the frames?
  • Resolution and data size: "4–10 s for a full stack" assumes an image size the prompt never gives. Every frame has to be downloaded to the judge's browser and held in memory. Decide what resolution the browser works at (binned? cropped?).
  • Export: is it linear or stretched, and at what bit depth? Also, a browser canvas can't write 16-bit PNG without an extra library.
  • Docker: say the container only serves static files, or an agent may build a backend.
  • Pages and back button: "calibration first" could mean an enforced order or just the layout order. For the back button, say how it should work (e.g. a hash route per step).
  • Rubric: only the Theme 1 description is included. If the rubric has scoring criteria, include them, because they should drive what gets cut.

4. Process

  • Milestone order: Milestone 0 builds the whole lesson before the image engine (Milestones 1–3). But the lesson's interactive pieces need that engine, so either they get faked or the engine gets built twice. The engine doesn't depend on Figma, so run it as a parallel track starting in Phase 1.
  • No timeboxes: "Iterate on Figma until we're both happy" plus "I have lots of input" is the biggest wall-clock risk in an 8-hour day. Give each phase a timebox and add an ordered cut list.
  • Model and effort policy: you say to be careful about models and thinking levels, but give no rule. Make one concrete, for example:
  • strongest model on high effort for the spec, calibration math and stacking algorithms;
  • a mid-tier model for building UI sections from Figma;
  • a small model for mechanical conversions;
  • section agents get only their section spec plus the shared contracts.
  • "Autonomous validation" has nothing to check against. Have Python reference implementations produce golden outputs on fixed ROI crops, require the JS to match them within a tolerance, and add a Playwright smoke test in Chrome and Firefox. Also give each milestone acceptance criteria.
  • Precompute depends on the spec: the precompute agent needs spec decisions (which masters to build). Start the uncontroversial parts right away: FWHM, star counts and histograms for every frame, and the flat masters.
  • Prose: writing it in Figma and then re-keying it into code is double work. Keep it in markdown files the app imports, with placeholders in Figma.
  • Persona: "passing familiarity with astrophotography" invites the model to defer to you, which is the opposite of what you want given item 2. Try: "strong grasp of the statistics and sensor physics; I'm the authority on practice; challenge me when something's wrong."
  • Ending: add "write the spec to SPEC.md, ask questions in one batch, and don't implement until I approve."

5. Typos that could leak into the app or file paths

  • xsif (twice) → xisf
  • ./source_iamges
  • "Satelite Trial Challenge": this will be copied verbatim onto a button.
  • "vigenetting" → "vignetting"
  • "let the user articulate it" → probably "adjust"

There are about 15 more cosmetic typos ("breif", "Milesone", "helds", and so on) that are harmless but worth a spell-check pass.

I can rewrite the prompt with these fixes. Where a decision is yours to make (the default algorithm, frame counts per challenge, the stretch baseline), I'd mark it as an open question rather than choose for you.
