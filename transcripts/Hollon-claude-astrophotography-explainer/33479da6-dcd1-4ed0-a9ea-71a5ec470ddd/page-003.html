Six items in §6.7 differ from what WBPP does. Only the first three change the result in a way you'd notice. Item 4 makes no visible difference, and items 5–6 already follow from your brief, so they're listed for completeness.

1. Local normalization → one normalization per frame (matters most)

  • WBPP: local normalization fits a background and scale model per frame on a grid of about 1,000 px. It matches each frame region by region to a reference built from the best frames. That flattens gradients that shift during the night, such as the sky changing with altitude or light pollution, and it partly evens out clouds.
  • Ours: one median and one scale per frame, precomputed for every calibration combination. Each frame is shifted and scaled as a whole to match the reference (y = (x − m_i)·(s_r/s_i) + m_r). This is exactly what WBPP does when local normalization is off.
  • Visible difference: backgrounds match less well where gradients differ between frames. Early vs late frames and East vs West frames are the likely cases. Rejection will also flag somewhat more pixels in those areas. The cloud frames keep their cloud structure, so rejection has to deal with it, and that's the lesson.
  • Why: faithful local normalization needs PSF photometry and multiscale background models. It would have to be precomputed for every frame × calibration combination × reference choice, which doesn't fit today.

2. PSF quality weights → equal weights

  • WBPP: each frame is weighted by a quality score. Your master used PSF SNR, with frame 14 at 1.00 and frame 16 at 0.245.
  • Ours: every selected frame counts equally. The learner's frame selection is the only weighting.
  • Visible difference: bad frames pull the average down more than they would in WBPP, so including them costs more. That makes the "judgement matters" lesson sharper, not weaker. A full stack comes out slightly noisier than a weighted one.
  • Why:
  • The synthetic frames have no WBPP weights.
  • The PSF weight equations are only published as images, so we couldn't confirm the exact formula.
  • With weights on, a bad frame's effect would be partly hidden, which muddies the average vs median vs rejection comparison.
  • We keep your recorded weights in the data if you'd like them shown in the frame list.

3. Automatic cosmetic correction → off

  • WBPP: it's on by default. During light calibration, any pixel more than 10 standard deviations above its local surroundings in the master dark is treated as hot and replaced with a value from its neighbours. Your master light's history doesn't say whether it was on for your run.
  • Ours: no replacement. Hot pixels left after dark subtraction are removed only by rejection across frames. Because your dark is 11 °C colder than the lights, a few hot pixels are under-corrected.
  • Visible difference: with plain average (no rejection), you may see a few residual hot pixels. With any rejection method they disappear. That demonstrates why rejection matters.
  • Easy to reverse: a precomputed hot-pixel map plus a 3×3 median replacement in the browser is small work. It could even be an optional toggle later.

4. Master flats: linear-fit clipping → winsorized clipping (no visible difference)

WBPP would pick linear-fit clipping automatically for 25 flats. Panel flats have essentially no outliers, so both methods keep nearly every pixel, and the masters differ only at the noise level.

5. Linear fit and ESD not offered (already your scope)

Your list has five algorithms, and your own master used linear fit. The Algorithms page will mention it as "what the author's master used" so the explanation stays honest.

6. No autocrop (already your requirement)

Your brief says edge gaps shouldn't count and the result shouldn't be autocropped.

Not listed because they give the same result: - Out-of-frame pixels are marked NaN rather than zero-and-range-reject. - There's no output pedestal. WBPP's automatic pedestal would be zero for these lights anyway. - Normalization statistics are measured before warping rather than after, on the frame minus a border.

Net effect: a workbench stack of your 20 frames won't be pixel-identical to your WBPP master. The differences will show mainly in how even the background is and slightly in noise.

Reply "approve all", or name the items you want changed. Item 3 is the cheapest to reverse if you'd like cosmetic correction included.
