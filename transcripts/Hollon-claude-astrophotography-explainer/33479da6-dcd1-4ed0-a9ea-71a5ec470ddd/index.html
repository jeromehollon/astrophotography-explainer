Findings that affect the spec

  • The registered _r.xisf frames are not calibrated. Dust and hot pixels are fixed to the sensor, so calibration has to happen before alignment. The browser pipeline will be: raw crop → calibrate → align (warp) using the matrix in each .xdrz → stack. The _r files then serve as a check on our alignment code.
  • Meridian flip: frames 0–7 were taken on one side of the pier and 8–19 on the other, rotated about 180°. The largest same-side offset is frames 2/3 vs 5, about 47 px. Frame 3 has the satellite trail, so I'd use 2 and 5 on the Alignment page.
  • The synthetic FITS frames are stored upside-down compared with the raw XISF files, because FITS stores rows bottom-up. The precompute will flip them before applying their untouched relatives' alignment.
  • What each bad frame contains: frame 11 is the tracking error (trailed stars), and frame 3's satellite trail is faint, in the lower right. Both match your brief.
  • The master dark already contains the bias: its median is 161 against 160.5 for the bias. Dark current on this sensor is negligible, and the dark mostly adds hot pixels.
  • The old dark is from an ASI294MM Pro at 8288×5644, so its size doesn't match our sensor and it can't be subtracted directly.
  • Flat test on the dust mote: the 50% flat removes it cleanly, the 10% flat nearly does, and the 85% flat leaves it visible. That gives the Flats–Continued page real evidence.

Questions

Each has my recommendation. Reply with the numbers you disagree with.

  1. Full-image resolution. At full resolution, 24 frames take about 1.25 GB of browser memory and about 700 MB to download. That won't work in a browser tab, and the download is too slow for judges hitting a VPS. I recommend ROIs at native resolution, from small precomputed crops, and the full stack and export at 2×2 binning (3112×2084). The full frames would load only when someone asks for a full stack, roughly 200 MB. 3×3 binning is the fallback if that's too heavy.
  2. Getting the data to the VPS. The binned frames are too large for git. I recommend building the image here in WSL and pushing it to a container registry, or using docker save. We'd commit the regeneration script and the small assets, not the frame data. How do you plan to deploy?
  3. Bias and dark both selected. I recommend the correct behaviour: remove the bias from the dark first, so the light isn't bias-subtracted twice. Bias then only matters for flats when dark flats are off. The alternative is to let the user make the double-subtraction mistake on purpose.
  4. The old-camera dark. I recommend showing it only on the Darks page as an amp-glow example, not as a selectable option. It could be resampled to fit, but that looks fake.
  5. Normalization before stacking. Sky background varies between frames: medians range from 783 to 1062 raw brightness units (ADU, internal only). Without normalization, the rejection algorithms misbehave. I recommend always on, explained in one sentence, with no toggle.
  6. Changing the reference frame. I recommend that the stack re-aligns to the chosen frame and the ROIs follow the sky, so picking a post-flip frame shows the field rotated 180°.

  8. ROI size. At native resolution the galaxy is about 2300×800 px, so a 512×512 ROI shows the core and the inner dust lanes but not the outer disk. What size do you want? My default is 512×512 for all four.

Decisions for the spec: - Bias and dark together: WBPP drops the bias whenever a master dark is present (dark optimization is off by default). The code comment says so directly: "MasterBias is needed only when … master dark is NOT present … or optimized." Light formula: C = (L − MD)/(MF/f). Bias is used only when there's no dark (L − MB). - Flats: each flat has the dark flat subtracted, or the bias if there's no dark flat, or nothing. The flats are then averaged with multiplicative normalization. - WBPP's automatic rejection would pick linear-fit clipping for 25 flats. I recommend winsorized clipping in our precompute; the difference is negligible for panel flats. - When a light is divided by the master flat, the flat is first scaled by its 5%-trimmed mean, so the light's overall level doesn't change. - Normalization: WBPP defaults to Local Normalization, which is too complex to run in the browser. Without it, WBPP uses one global formula, y = (x − m_i)·(s_ref/s_i) + m_ref, where m is the median and s is the square root of the biweight midvariance. That same formula works for rejection and for combining. - Precompute (m, s) for 24 frames × 30 calibration combinations, which is 720 pairs. - The 30 combinations are {none, bias, dark} × {no flat, 9 master flats}. Bias plus dark gives the same result as dark alone. - Rejection defaults WBPP sets: kappa-sigma 4/3; winsorized 4/3 with cutoff 5; robust Chauvenet limit 0.1 (the PCL default is 0.5); linear fit 5/3.5; percentile 0.2/0.1. - Automatic choice: fewer than 6 frames → percentile; 15 or fewer, or bias/dark → winsorized; otherwise linear fit. - Out-of-frame pixels are rejected when their value is ≤ 0. We'll use NaN for the same job. - The doc copies the exact winsorized and Chauvenet algorithms from the installed header files, down to the constants. - Weighting: the WBPP default is PSF Signal Weight. The master light used PSF SNR, which you chose. I recommend equal weights in our pipeline and showing the recorded WBPP weights for display only; they're listed per frame in the doc. - Registration: Lanczos-3 with clamping 0.3. The matrix maps reference coordinates to raw-frame coordinates, and the header docs confirm the origin at 0.5 means pixel centres sit on whole numbers. - The doc has the composition formula for re-referencing to any frame and for 2×2 binning. - Automatic cosmetic correction is on by default in WBPP. I recommend skipping it.

No size limit. The coordinate parameters are optional now, and leaving them out returns the whole image. The server streams rows straight from disk and sends Content-Length, so a full-frame request doesn't use extra server memory, and the full-stack button can show download progress.

Sensor space or canonical space. I recommend sensor space, and that's what the spec now says. The deciding reason is that calibration has to happen before alignment, because dust, hot pixels and vignetting are fixed to the sensor, not the sky.

  • With sensor space, the client asks for the same sensor rectangle from the light and from each master. It calibrates them with simple per-pixel maths, then warps. The server just reads pixels.
  • With canonical space, three things go wrong:
  • The server would have to resample images, which moves processing to the backend.
  • Every master (bias, dark, flat) would have to be warped separately for each frame, which is roughly four times the data sent.
  • Calibration would effectively run after the warp. Dividing by the flat and Lanczos clamping give different answers in that order.

The only place that converts a canonical ROI into sensor rectangles is one client function (the footprint step in §4.4). The server knows nothing about geometry.

#112026-09-24T15:52:45.344Z

Explain the deviations from WBPP that require my approval?

Six items in §6.7 differ from what WBPP does. Only the first three change the result in a way you'd notice. Item 4 makes no visible difference, and items 5–6 already follow from your brief, so they're listed for completeness.

1. Local normalization → one normalization per frame (matters most)

  • WBPP: local normalization fits a background and scale model per frame on a grid of about 1,000 px. It matches each frame region by region to a reference built from the best frames. That flattens gradients that shift during the night, such as the sky changing with altitude or light pollution, and it partly evens out clouds.
  • Ours: one median and one scale per frame, precomputed for every calibration combination. Each frame is shifted and scaled as a whole to match the reference (y = (x − m_i)·(s_r/s_i) + m_r). This is exactly what WBPP does when local normalization is off.
  • Visible difference: backgrounds match less well where gradients differ between frames. Early vs late frames and East vs West frames are the likely cases. Rejection will also flag somewhat more pixels in those areas. The cloud frames keep their cloud structure, so rejection has to deal with it, and that's the lesson.
  • Why: faithful local normalization needs PSF photometry and multiscale background models. It would have to be precomputed for every frame × calibration combination × reference choice, which doesn't fit today.

2. PSF quality weights → equal weights

  • WBPP: each frame is weighted by a quality score. Your master used PSF SNR, with frame 14 at 1.00 and frame 16 at 0.245.
  • Ours: every selected frame counts equally. The learner's frame selection is the only weighting.
  • Visible difference: bad frames pull the average down more than they would in WBPP, so including them costs more. That makes the "judgement matters" lesson sharper, not weaker. A full stack comes out slightly noisier than a weighted one.
  • Why:
  • The synthetic frames have no WBPP weights.
  • The PSF weight equations are only published as images, so we couldn't confirm the exact formula.
  • With weights on, a bad frame's effect would be partly hidden, which muddies the average vs median vs rejection comparison.
  • We keep your recorded weights in the data if you'd like them shown in the frame list.

3. Automatic cosmetic correction → off

  • WBPP: it's on by default. During light calibration, any pixel more than 10 standard deviations above its local surroundings in the master dark is treated as hot and replaced with a value from its neighbours. Your master light's history doesn't say whether it was on for your run.
  • Ours: no replacement. Hot pixels left after dark subtraction are removed only by rejection across frames. Because your dark is 11 °C colder than the lights, a few hot pixels are under-corrected.
  • Visible difference: with plain average (no rejection), you may see a few residual hot pixels. With any rejection method they disappear. That demonstrates why rejection matters.
  • Easy to reverse: a precomputed hot-pixel map plus a 3×3 median replacement in the browser is small work. It could even be an optional toggle later.

4. Master flats: linear-fit clipping → winsorized clipping (no visible difference)

WBPP would pick linear-fit clipping automatically for 25 flats. Panel flats have essentially no outliers, so both methods keep nearly every pixel, and the masters differ only at the noise level.

5. Linear fit and ESD not offered (already your scope)

Your list has five algorithms, and your own master used linear fit. The Algorithms page will mention it as "what the author's master used" so the explanation stays honest.

6. No autocrop (already your requirement)

Your brief says edge gaps shouldn't count and the result shouldn't be autocropped.

Not listed because they give the same result: - Out-of-frame pixels are marked NaN rather than zero-and-range-reject. - There's no output pedestal. WBPP's automatic pedestal would be zero for these lights anyway. - Normalization statistics are measured before warping rather than after, on the frame minus a border.

Net effect: a workbench stack of your 20 frames won't be pixel-identical to your WBPP master. The differences will show mainly in how even the background is and slightly in noise.

Reply "approve all", or name the items you want changed. Item 3 is the cheapest to reverse if you'd like cosmetic correction included.
